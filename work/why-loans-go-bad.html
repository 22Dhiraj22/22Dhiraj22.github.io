Why loans go bad: a study for the CEO that became a product

The CEO asked what our bad loans in one rural lending pool had in common. The goal was not to recover them, but to learn what to stop doing. I compared them with a control group of good loans, and the clearest early signal pointed at payments, not at the borrower's profile.

01 · The questionWhat do our bad loans have in common?

About 150 loans in the pool had gone bad, most of them to dairy farmers. Leadership had several theories: seasonality, borrowers with no credit history, high repayment burden, and the idea that loans fail only after several EMIs. Each theory pointed to a different fix.

I agreed one rule with the CEO up front: this is about process and policy, not about chasing these borrowers. That kept the study from drifting into a recovery list.

02 · MethodCompare like with like

  1. A control group. Bad loans compared with about 1,200 good loans of the same type, booked in the same months. Without this, every pattern in the bad loans looks meaningful.
  2. Read-only data access. I asked for a read-only database user so the analysis could never change live data.
  3. Hypotheses first. Each leadership theory became a testable question with a yes or no answer.
  4. A simple risk score at the end, to check how much of the outcome the data can explain at all.

03 · What we foundThe early signal was in payments

  • Auto-debit at the first EMI. Mandates were registered equally often for good and bad loans (~91%). But the first EMI was actually collected by auto-debit for 59% of good loans and only 31% of bad ones. Loans whose first EMI missed auto-debit went bad about 3× as often.
  • Stress shows up by EMI 2–3. By the third EMI, 70% of loans that later went bad had already been late at least once, against 29% of good loans. The "fails after 7 EMIs" theory didn't hold.
  • Credit risk signals mattered, at the edges. Low bureau scores, repayment burden above half of income, and high loan-to-value each roughly doubled the odds.
  • Borrowers with no credit history did not fail more. They did slightly better, which argued against tightening policy for first-time borrowers.
  • A small group of branches held about a third of the bad loans while doing an eighth of the lending. The data couldn't explain why, so the next step is a file-by-file review of matched pairs.

The simple risk score separated good from bad only moderately (AUC ~0.7), but the riskiest 20% of loans held about half of the bad ones. I said plainly in the write-up that most of the variation is not in our data, so nobody would treat the model as a verdict.

04 · From insight to productProve the mandate works before paying out

If a working auto-debit at the first EMI is the strongest early signal, the cheapest fix is to make sure it works before disbursal. I wrote the epic for a ₹1 test debit on the registered mandate, placed right after the pre-disbursal call:

Mandate registered
→
₹1 test debit
→
Pass: disburse
/
Fail: fix mandate first

I kept the first version small: one mandate at a time with attempt history, and no guarantor section or bulk runs. It answers one question for the branch: will the first EMI actually come through?

05 · Communicating itOne analysis, two audiences

  • For the CEO: the full findings, the method and the limits.
  • For field leadership: a short, neutral deck with no branch names and rounded policy thresholds, written as "1 in N" so it would start a conversation instead of a blame session.

06 · LearningsWhat I'd keep doing

  • Always build the control group. Half the "patterns" in the bad loans were just as common in the good ones.
  • Say what the data can't tell you. It made the findings more credible, not less.
  • An insight is finished when it changes a process. The mandate check is worth more than the deck.
